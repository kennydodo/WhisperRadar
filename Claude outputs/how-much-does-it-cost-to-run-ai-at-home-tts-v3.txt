Running AI at home sounds free. You buy the computer once, and after that, every answer costs nothing. Is that true?

Not quite. Running AI at home has two costs: a one-time cost for the hardware, and a running cost for the electricity. In the examples we'll look at, the hardware was the bigger part. And whether home beats a hosted service depends mostly on one thing: how much you use it.

Let's start with a made-up example. Dana writes with AI every day. She's thinking about buying a used graphics card for six hundred dollars, so she can run models on her own computer instead of paying a hosted service. Her question is simple. Which one is cheaper? To answer it, she needs one number she can compare: what it costs her to produce a million tokens of text. A token is a small piece of a word, roughly three quarters of a word in English. By the end of this video, you'll be able to work out that number for your own setup, with a calculator and a few minutes of measuring.

We'll build it in three parts. First, electricity. Then the hardware. Then the comparison. Let's begin with electricity, because it's the part that feels hardest to guess.

Electricity is billed in kilowatt hours. Here's what that means. A watt is a rate of using power. A kilowatt is a thousand watts. If something draws one thousand watts for one hour, it has used one kilowatt hour. A one hundred watt light bulb left on for ten hours also uses one kilowatt hour.

So the formula has three pieces. Watts, times hours, divided by one thousand, gives kilowatt hours. Then kilowatt hours times your price per kilowatt hour gives money.

What's the price? It depends on where you live, and your electricity bill shows yours. For a rough national figure in the United States, the Energy Information Administration's October twenty twenty-six outlook projects an average residential price of about eighteen cents per kilowatt hour for this year. Yours may be higher or lower. Dana will use eighteen cents, because it's a convenient round number.

Now the watts. Here's a detail that catches people out: a computer doesn't draw one fixed amount of power. It draws a lot while the model is writing, and much less while it sits waiting. So you need two numbers, the load power while writing, and the idle power while waiting.

Before you decide, here is what the arithmetic leaves out.

First, quality. The comparison only makes sense if the model you can run at home does the job as well as the hosted one you're pricing. A cheaper model that does the task badly isn't a saving. Compare two models that give you answers you'd accept.

Second, your time. Setting up, updating and fixing a home machine takes hours, and those hours have a value to you.

Third, reasons other than cost. Some people run models at home for privacy, or so the tool works without an internet connection. Those can be reasons to choose home even when hosted is cheaper.

Now let's turn this into a method you can use today. Five steps.

Step one. Find your price per kilowatt hour on your electricity bill.

Step two. Decide which electricity counts. If the computer would be on anyway, count only the extra. If you leave it on for AI, count the waiting power too. Measure with a plug-in meter, once while it's idle, and again while the model works.

Step three. Measure your speed on a real session, with the prompts and documents you actually use, including the time spent reading them. Some apps show reading speed separately. If yours doesn't, time the whole session with a clock.

Step four. Count how long the model is actually working during a typical day.

Step five. Add up the total monthly cost of the same tasks both ways. At home, that's the hardware divided by the months you expect to use it, plus the electricity from your meter readings. For hosted AI, include what you send and what you get back. Then compare the two totals, and divide by the work done if you want a cost per million tokens.
